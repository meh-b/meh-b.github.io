Mental health

Complex post-traumatic stress disorder (cPTSD)

Transparency and embarrassment

I think before going in on the deep end it would make the most sense to really explain why at all would I be public about my experience with cPTSD and why would that even be relevant in the public sphere. Perhaps even before that, it’d be of use to even explain what on earth cPTSD is and how it differs from PTSD itself, which is much more commonly portrayed in Hollywood movies via the “shell-shocked” soldier.

So let me first start with the clinical definition as given by the Cleveland Clinic: “Complex post-traumatic stress disorder (CPTSD) can result from experiencing chronic trauma, such as prolonged child abuse or domestic violence.” Fairly straightforward but as usual, much of the flavor and the understanding of the experience of cPTSD is completely removed via medical jargon. Strangely, before the concept was formalized medically, Toni Morrison characterized it much more precisely in her book The Bluest Eye.

As you can tell from the text then, an experience with some of the most intense forms of cPTSD involves the murder of one’s sense of self. Self-preservation is murdered, a narrative of self is murdered, any concept of the id is destroyed. When coupled to the fact that rates of cPTSD can range anywhere from 6–10% of the populace, it becomes an extremely important social component to become aware of. This has been my affliction with respect to mental illness.

Though that may answer the question of why cPTSD is important at all though, it doesn’t answer as to why I’d be public with it at all. It has been a part of myself that I was at one point, quite ashamed of. Ashamed that I even thought of it, that I felt the need to speak about it and ultimately that I’d be viewed as someone who whines or pretends to be hurt by discussing it. And that is the cPTSD doing its work, inserting doubt, gaslighting me and making me think that perhaps all that I went through was not that bad.

So why discuss it at all? Well during my childhood, I remember mentioning the fact that I was hit, almost as though it was a quotidian fact, to classmates. When I returned home, I was told to not discuss this at all in front of others. As a result, I kept a part of myself always hidden, that it was not right to discuss what went on in the home in the public sphere. That abuse continued until the age of 20 and my mind was broken many times over by then. However, to the contrary, nothing could’ve been more right to discuss openly. When I went into the sciences for my undergraduate and graduate degrees, I completely shoved these experiences down. But it was extremely difficult.

How the abuse happened

My father was a mathematician, in fact a reasonably well known one. When I was a child, at about the age of 4–5, I asked him with a strong insistence that I wanted to start studying mathematics and physics. Something in me knew that it was a topic that I was heavily interested in and it wasn’t because I knew my father did it. I think there was something in hearing the languages of equations that my older sisters spoke in, in the midst of their own studies, that captured my interest strongly.

From then on, I’d receive lessons in the two subjects and be given problems to do. Unfortunately, when I was unable to resolve problems was often where much of the abuse arose. I’d often be hit and insulted for not being able to do those problems. I remember times when I even did problems correctly, and my father seemed to want to take his anger out on me anyways (this one time I explained to him why double bonds in chemistry had to be shorter in length compared to single bonds; he gaslit me into thinking I was wrong and beat me). Even when uncontrollably crying, the logic was that nothing like a good slap and a few more insults would get me to focus. I’d often dread the moments he walked through the door, coming home from work, or from wherever else. I’d often be able to read his mood from how he walked around and I’d remain hyper-vigilant to avoid ever angering him.

He continued with that abuse even outside the context of mathematics and physics. Even when I did something that was not to his liking, such as being too “American” or too “careless” or not “hard-working” or whatever else it may have been, I was castigated and psychologically/physically abused. Every moment had to be filled with optimized action. I had to be studying constantly. I remember when I took a break from studying shortly after finishing my last AP exams in high school. I had set a record for the school taking 12 AP courses and received top marks in all of them. During that break, my father became furious with me and called me an “old man” for resting.

When I was older (12–13 and beyond) and resisted a little more, trying to avoid being hit, or trying to lock myself in my room, or if I was cornered in a room and tried to simply walk past my father, I was often told I was being violent, that I was a delinquent, that I’d go to jail. My interactions with “friends” were always heavily controlled and supervised. A few other times, I recall being spat on. I rarely ever got to feel free and whatever experiences I did manage to have that allowed me to feel like a child were only thanks to my mother, who was also abused and sacrificed herself for me and my sisters.

When I was 22, my father passed away, leaving all of it unresolved and for me to pick up the pieces. As you can imagine, this was not conducive to my own mental well being, nor was it helpful for the future of my own life. If anything, it produced a poison, in which I started to become unable to tell what parts of math and physics were oppression and hatred, and which parts were love. Naturally, it made me suicidal for a long period of time. And it has impacted my career in research. It made it often very difficult for me to focus on concepts that you would expect for me to be comfortable with; but as a result of trauma, I was fearful of, even though, it is what I love.

During graduate school, I was not in an environment that was conducive to healing and many of the people around me did not understand just how “exceptional” my experiences were. So that’s why this has to be discussed in the open sphere. Because if not, then when or how? What kind of world do I live in, if a large part of who I am, is not allowed to be seen? Regardless of that, as many during graduate school can attest, I worked long and hard hours without being allowed to publish unfortunately.

Healing

In 2024, I had a mental breakdown. I was applying for postdocs without any publications despite producing a mountain of work and as a result I was forced to be unemployed for a year. During that year I also developed an eating disorder and would run 100 miles/week often. I was desperate for a change, to escape from constantly feeling that there was just something fundamentally wrong with me, and did not know how I was going to manage to keep carrying on living. I still did not grow any self-esteem at the age of 29. Many days, I'd have too much anxiety and self-doubt and it'd result in pain in my nervous system that would render me completely catatonic at times. However, I thankfully had the support of my partner, but in many ways this was an internal battle that raged on for decades before I ever met her and as a result, I felt that I still faced it alone. My quality of life and well-being was extremely poor. But it was her who recognized that those were the symptoms I had.

I was diagnosed with cPTSD in 2025 and likely some version of borderline personality disorder/autism (I was not vocal till the age of 5 and often had to mask myself around my family). By then I started receiving EMDR and Deep Brain Reorienting. These therapies in combination with some Wellbutrin, are what allowed me to finally start piecing together the different parts of me that were so deeply shattered as a child. One of the few things that kept me going was also political advocacy on behalf of other children being traumatized around the world. As a result, I’ve developed a more encompassing view of the world that I live in beyond my own studies, perhaps becoming more whole.

In many ways, I have still been recovering from my mental breakdown. But my postdoc advisers have been the first people to understand that I needed time to heal in a professional setting. For that I am incredibly indebted to them. My partner, my mother and my best friend from graduate school, have all been supportive of me and my process of healing. It’s been extremely difficult, riddled with doubt, lack of a will to live, and difficult moments in which I’ve had to confront myself. If it was Toni Morrison, a powerful black female writer who identified the problem so clearly and ahead of her time, it was Audre Lorde who so clearly found the antidote.

Several of her quotes come to mind:

We are powerful because we have survived, and that is what it is all about — survival and growth.

I was going to die, sooner or later, whether or not I had even spoken myself. My silences had not protected me. Your silences will not protect you.... What are the words you do not yet have? What are the tyrannies you swallow day by day and attempt to make your own, until you will sicken and die of them, still in silence? We have been socialized to respect fear more than our own need for language.

These two quotes, among others, made me cry outside of being beat, for the third time in my life. She gave me the words to speak of my own survival, the moments I thought of ending it all when I was 12 or 21... but didn’t and carried on.

That’s why I feel the need to speak out on cPTSD. I personally feel that too often the world readily throws away the children it damaged. And I refuse that and will advocate for myself and others who have suffered in ways that quite frankly, most people never will understand on an experiential level.
